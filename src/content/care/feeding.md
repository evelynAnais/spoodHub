## Prey size

The usual guidance is **no larger than the spider's own abdomen**, and slightly smaller is
safer than slightly larger. An oversized cricket can injure a jumping spider, particularly one
that is approaching a molt and less inclined to fight.

## What to offer

| Prey | Best for | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Drosophila melanogaster* | Spiderlings, tiny species | Flightless, small, easy to culture |
| *Drosophila hydei* | Larger spiderlings, juveniles | Bigger than melanogaster, still flightless |
| Blue bottle fly | Juveniles to adults | Close to ideal — nutritious and a strong feeding response |
| House fly | Juveniles to adults | Same idea, slightly smaller |
| Cricket | Juveniles to adults | Feed only appropriately sized ones; never leave uneaten |
| Dubia roach nymph | Juveniles to adults | Easy to keep, but less of a feeding response |
| Waxworm | Occasional treat | Very fatty — not a staple |
| Mealworm | Occasional | Hard exoskeleton, poor nutrition ratio; not recommended as a staple |

Vary the prey where you can. A single-item diet is workable but not ideal.

## How often

| Stage | Frequency |
| --- | --- |
| Spiderling | Every 1–2 days |
| Juvenile | Every 2–3 days |
| Adult | Every 3–4 days |
| Pre-molt | Do not offer |
| Post-molt | Wait 3–5 days |

These are starting points, not rules. A spider with a plump abdomen can wait; a visibly thin
one should be offered food sooner.

## Reading the abdomen

The abdomen is the fuel gauge. Plump and rounded means well fed. Shrunken and wrinkled means
underfed or dehydrated — check the water situation first, since dehydration looks similar and
is more urgent.

A very large, tight abdomen in a spider that has stopped eating is more likely pre-molt than
overfeeding.

## Always remove uneaten live prey

This matters more than it sounds. A cricket left in an enclosure overnight will chew on a
spider, and a spider that is molting cannot defend itself. Interrupted molts are frequently
fatal. If prey is not taken within a few hours, take it out.

## Why refusals get logged

A refused feeding is not a non-event — it is the single most useful data point for predicting a
molt. Three refusals in a row from a spider that normally eats readily is the classic sign that
a molt is coming.

That is why the tracker asks whether prey was accepted rather than just whether you fed. A log
of meals alone cannot tell you anything; a log that includes the refusals can.
