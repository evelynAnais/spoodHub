If you keep a female jumping spider for long enough, one day she will seal herself into a thick
silk chamber and stop coming out. This page is about telling apart the two things that might
mean, and about the fact that a female who has never met a male can still lay eggs.

## A female with no mate can still produce an egg sac

This surprises almost every new keeper, and it is worth stating plainly: **producing an egg sac
does not mean your spider was mated.** An unmated female can build a sac and lay a full clutch
of eggs into it. Those eggs are simply infertile, and nothing will hatch.

From the outside the two are essentially identical. A sac from an unmated female looks the same,
is guarded just as fiercely, and costs her the same effort.

There is a second route to a surprise sac, and it is the more common one for a wild-caught
spider. After mating, a female stores sperm in her **spermathecae** — paired internal
receptacles — and can draw on it later. The gap between mating and fertilising eggs can run to
months. So a female caught outdoors as an adult may produce a fertile sac long after arriving in
your care, with no male anywhere in the picture.

Two practical consequences:

- **A captive-bred female raised alone → the sac is almost certainly infertile.**
- **A wild-caught adult female → the sac may well be fertile, even months in.** Plan for
  spiderlings before they arrive, not after.

If you are not equipped to raise dozens of spiderlings, that second case is the one to think
about ahead of time.

## Egg sac or molt retreat?

Both are thick silk chambers the spider seals herself into and stops eating in. The distinction
matters because one resolves itself in a couple of weeks and the other produces up to a few
hundred spiders.

| | Molt retreat | Egg sac |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who builds it | Any spider, any age, either sex | Adult females only |
| Shape | Follows the spider's own outline — a flattened tube or hammock | Rounder and lumpier, often with a visible mass inside |
| The spider | Inside it, alone | Usually sitting *on* or beside it, guarding |
| Comes out | Emerges after a few days to two weeks, next to a shed skin | Stays with it, may build more sacs later |
| The giveaway | An **exuvia** — a shed exoskeleton — left behind | No exuvia; she is still the same size |

**The shed skin is the reliable tell.** A molt leaves an exuvia; an egg sac does not. If she
emerges thinner with a discarded copy of herself next to her, that was a molt.

The other strong signal is where she is. A molting spider is sealed *inside*. A female guarding
eggs is typically outside the sac or wrapped around it, and will be far more defensive than
usual about the enclosure being opened.

## Recognising a gravid female

Before a sac appears there are usually a few weeks of warning:

- **A steadily enlarging abdomen** that keeps growing rather than shrinking between meals.
- **Increased appetite**, then a sharp drop just before sac construction.
- **Heavy silking** in one corner, more than a normal retreat needs.
- **Reduced roaming.** She settles into one area and stays there.

The abdomen change is the clearest, but it overlaps with pre-molt — which is exactly why the
tracker asks you to log refusals and webbing separately rather than guessing.

## What to do

**Mostly nothing, again.**

- **Do not open the sac.** Not to count, not to check fertility. There is no way to inspect it
  that improves the outcome.
- **Keep feeding her.** A guarding female often eats little, but should still be offered prey at
  her normal interval. Remove anything uneaten.
- **Do not rehouse her.** Moving a guarding female is stressful and she may abandon or destroy
  the sac.
- **Maintain steady humidity.** Eggs are more sensitive to drying than the adult is.

If eggs are fertile, spiderlings typically emerge inside the sac, go through a first molt in
there, and only then break out — so the sac may sit apparently inert for weeks before anything
visible happens.

## If it hatches

A single *Phidippus* sac can contain well over a hundred spiderlings, and they are cannibalistic
almost immediately. You will need separate containers and a supply of very small prey
(*Drosophila melanogaster*) far sooner than feels reasonable.

Plan for that before pairing any spiders. See the breeding guide when it exists — and if you
have raised a clutch, that guide badly needs your firsthand account.

## If you are unsure

The honest answer for a keeper looking at a lump of silk is that a **week of patience settles
it**. A molt resolves quickly and leaves evidence. An egg sac does not. Neither situation is
improved by opening anything.
