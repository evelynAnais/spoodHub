Jumping spiders are the most readable spiders in the hobby, because almost everything they do is
driven by sight. Learn what they are looking at and with which eyes, and most of their behavior
stops being mysterious.

## A note on "happy"

There is no way to measure whether a spider is happy, and anyone claiming otherwise is guessing.
What you *can* assess is whether one is **thriving or stressed** — and those have observable
signs. This guide sticks to those.

A thriving jumping spider eats readily, moves around its enclosure rather than hiding constantly,
grooms itself, builds a tidy retreat, molts on schedule, and orients toward movement with
curiosity rather than fleeing it. That is the realistic target.

## They have two visual systems, doing different jobs

This is the single most useful thing to understand, and it has direct consequences for how you
handle them.

**The principal eyes** — the two huge forward-facing ones — have extremely high acuity and
movable retinas behind fixed lenses. These are for *examining* things: identifying prey, mates,
and you. This is the pair that makes a jumping spider appear to look at you, because it is.

**The secondary eyes** — the smaller ones spread around the sides and top — have lower
resolution but a much wider field of view and far better motion detection. These are the alarm
system.

Spano and colleagues tested which pair actually triggers escape from a looming object, using
*Phidippus audax*. The result is unambiguous:

- with the **anterior lateral (side) eyes masked**, only **14.3%** of spiders backed away
- with those eyes working, **81%** backed away
- masking the **principal eyes made no difference at all** to this response

**Your approaching hand is detected by the side eyes, not the big front ones.** A hand coming in
from above or the side registers as a looming shape in the motion-detection system before the
spider has had any chance to examine what it is. That is why a spider that was calmly watching
you suddenly bolts when you reach in.

## Reading defensive behavior

Scriba and colleagues catalogued how jumping spiders respond to a perceived predator. The named
behaviors map neatly onto what keepers see:

| Behavior | What it looks like | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Freezing** | Complete stillness, ten seconds or more | Threat noticed at a distance; hoping not to be seen |
| **Directional change** | Turning to face away and move off | Disengaging |
| **Retreat** | Slow, deliberate backward walking | Threat is closer; withdrawing while watching |
| **Hiding** | Quick dash under or behind cover | Threat close enough for active escape |
| **Jumping down** | Launching off the surface entirely | Last resort — and how spiders get lost |

The important pattern: **response depends on distance.** Freezing dominated when the threat was
far away, active escape when it was near. So a spider that freezes as you approach has already
seen you. Continuing to advance escalates it toward a bolt or a jump.

A spider held high above a hard floor that reaches the jumping-down stage is the most common way
one gets injured or lost. Work low, over a soft surface.

### Sex and age change the picture

The same study found adult males behaved markedly differently: they let threats get significantly
closer before reacting, froze less, and — remarkably — **responded to predator stimuli with
courtship displays**, leg-waving and leaning, rather than defense. Females and juveniles were
consistently more cautious and reacted from further away.

If your mature male seems bolder than your female, that is a real and documented difference, not
your imagination. It is not necessarily a sign he is more relaxed.

**A caveat, as ever:** this work used *Saitis barbipes*, not *Phidippus*. Same family, different
genus — so treat it as a strong expectation rather than a measured fact about your regal. See
[where jumping spiders fit](/classification) for why that distinction matters.

## Signs of a stressed spider

Not all of these are individually documented; several are widely reported keeper observation and
are flagged as such. Taken together they are still the practical picture:

- **Persistent hiding** in a spider that previously explored
- **Fleeing rather than orienting** when you approach the enclosure
- **Refusing food** with no pre-molt signs and no recent rehouse
  (see [molting](/care/molting) — refusal has several causes)
- **Frantic climbing** of the enclosure walls, especially repeatedly at the same spot
- **A defensive posture** — front legs raised, body lowered, facing you

That last one is the closest thing to "angry." It is a warning, not aggression: the spider is
telling you it feels cornered. The correct response is to withdraw and leave it alone, not to
push through.

## Approaching without frightening one

Everything here follows from the two-eye-systems finding above.

**Move slowly.** The secondary eyes are motion detectors. Slow movement produces far less of a
looming signal than a fast one, whatever the size.

**Approach from the front, low and level.** Coming in from the front gives the principal eyes a
chance to examine and identify you before the alarm system decides. Coming from above is a
predator's approach angle.

**Let the spider see your hand before it arrives.** Resting a hand still nearby for a few moments
lets the spider look at it properly. Many will then walk onto it of their own accord — which is
always better than picking one up.

**Never grab, pinch, or trap one against a surface.** Aside from the injury risk, it guarantees
the defensive response.

**Work at desk height over a soft surface.** Assume at some point it will jump.

**Keep sessions short**, and put it back before it starts trying to escape rather than after.

**Do not handle at all when the spider is in pre-molt, has just molted, or has just been
rehoused.** All three are already stressful.

## What reliably stresses them

- **Vibration.** Enclosures on a speaker, a washing machine, or a frequently slammed door.
- **Being moved.** Rehousing costs most spiders a few feedings — the tracker accounts for this
  so it is not mistaken for pre-molt.
- **Constant light,** or no day-night cycle at all. See [sleep and rest](/care/sleep-and-rest).
- **Being watched from above** through a clear lid, which is exactly the looming-predator angle.
- **An enclosure with nowhere to retreat.** A spider with no retreat has no way to opt out of
  being observed, and that alone is a stressor.

The last one is worth dwelling on: much of what reads as a "friendly" spider is really a spider
that feels secure enough not to need to hide. Giving it a good retreat tends to make it *more*
visible, not less.
