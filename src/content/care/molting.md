A spider grows by shedding its exoskeleton. It is the most dangerous routine event in its life
and the point at which keeper interference does the most damage.

## Recognising pre-molt

No single sign is conclusive. Several together are.

- **Refusing food.** The most reliable early signal. Two or three refusals in a row from a
  spider that normally eats is worth acting on.
- **Heavy webbing.** The retreat gets noticeably thicker and denser than usual.
- **Sealing the retreat.** The spider closes itself in completely. This is the strongest sign
  there is — at this point a molt is imminent or already underway.
- **Dulled colours.** Markings look washed out or greyish as the new cuticle forms underneath.
- **A large, tight abdomen** without recent feeding to explain it.
- **Lethargy** and reduced interest in movement outside the enclosure.

### What else refused food can mean

Refusing prey is the most useful pre-molt signal, but it is not exclusive to molting. Before
concluding a molt is coming, rule out:

- **A recent rehouse.** Being moved is stressful and commonly costs a few feedings. If the
  refusals started right after a move and there is no thickening silk, the move is the simpler
  explanation.
- **Too-large prey.** A spider will often refuse an item it considers a bad fight.
- **Temperature.** A cool spider eats less. Appetite frequently returns with a few degrees.
- **A full abdomen.** A spider that ate well two days ago may simply not be hungry.
- **Illness or injury**, which needs attention rather than a paused feeding schedule.

## What to do

Almost nothing, deliberately.

1. **Stop offering prey** and remove anything live still in the enclosure.
2. **Keep humidity steady.** A slightly more humid enclosure helps, but do not soak it and do
   not mist the retreat directly.
3. **Do not open, move, or rearrange** the enclosure.
4. **Wait.** Depending on size and temperature, a spider may stay sealed in for a few days to
   over two weeks. This is normal and is not a reason to check on it.

## What not to do

**Never disturb a molting spider.** Not to look, not to move it, not to "help". A spider
interrupted mid-molt frequently dies, and one that survives often loses a limb or is left with
a deformity. There is no situation in which intervening improves the outcome.

**Do not remove the exuvia immediately.** The shed skin is harmless. Take it out later, once
the spider is out and active again.

## After the molt

The new exoskeleton is soft and the fangs need to harden before the spider can eat safely.

- **Wait 3–5 days before offering food.** Larger species take longer. Offering prey to a
  soft-fanged spider risks damaging the fangs.
- **Expect brighter colours** — post-molt is when markings look best.
- **Water is fine** immediately.

## Molt intervals

Intervals lengthen with each instar. Spiderlings may molt every 10–14 days; a large juvenile
might go 30–45 days; an adult female may molt once more or not at all. Temperature matters a
great deal — a warmer spider eats more and molts sooner.

Published intervals are only ever a starting point. Once you have recorded two molts for an
individual spider, that spider's own history is a far better predictor than any table, which is
why the tracker switches over to it as soon as it can.

## When it goes wrong

A spider stuck mid-molt — partly out of its old skin and no longer progressing after many
hours — is in serious trouble, and usually the cause is an enclosure that was too dry. Raising
humidity is the only thing worth trying, and the outcome is often poor regardless. Prevention
is the whole game: steady humidity, good ventilation, and no interference.

A spider that refuses food for weeks without molting, has a shrunken abdomen, or shows a
lasting change in posture is not in pre-molt and deserves a closer look at temperature,
hydration, and enclosure conditions.
