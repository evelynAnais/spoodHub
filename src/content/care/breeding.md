Breeding jumping spiders is not difficult to start. That is the problem. Putting two adults
together is a five-minute decision that can commit you to several hundred individually housed
spiders over the following six months, and the hard part arrives long after the interesting part
is over.

This page is mostly about the arithmetic. If you read one section, read the next one.

## What one pairing commits you to

In the study that documents this best, each female was mated **exactly once**. From that single
mating, fertile females went on to lay a series of **one to five fertile batches of eggs**,
roughly a month apart, over the following months.

So the commitment is not one egg sac. It is potentially five, from one pairing, spread across
half a year — and you do not find out how many until she stops.

Batch sizes decline as she goes. The fitted trend across those batches runs from roughly **81
eggs in the first down to around 33 in the fifth**, and the proportion that actually hatch falls
with each batch too. Those figures are for *Phidippus johnsoni*, a smaller species than the
regals most keepers have; expect a large *Phidippus regius* to lay more per batch, not fewer.

Take the low end seriously anyway. Two successful batches of eighty is a hundred and sixty
spiderlings, and they are cannibalistic almost immediately, which means individual containers.
See [egg sacs and gravid females](/care/egg-sacs) for what an egg sac is and how to recognize
one.

**Do the container arithmetic before you pair, not after she lays.**

## Both spiders have to be adult — and the male gets there first

You cannot pair a subadult. A male's pedipalps are only functional after his final molt, and a
female has no external genital opening before hers. Working out where each spider is in that
sequence is the whole subject of [telling males from females](/care/sexing).

The complication is that **males mature earlier than females**, in fewer molts. In the
laboratory, *P. johnsoni* males matured in 5 to 7 molts against 6 to 8 for females; spiders in
the wild took roughly one more molt each. Males also reach a smaller adult size and have shorter
adult lives.

Two practical consequences:

- **A male from the same clutch will usually mature well before his sisters**, and adult males do
  not live long. A male can age out of usefulness while you wait.
- **Pairing across clutches, or across sources, is often the only way the timing works.**

## What courtship actually looks like

Males do not simply approach. In *P. johnsoni*, males use one of three tactics depending on where
the female is and how mature she is:

| The female is | What the male does |
| --- | --- |
| **Adult, out in the open** | Visual display — gesturing with the forelegs, dancing in front of her. Depends entirely on her seeing him |
| **Adult, inside her nest** | Tugging, probing and vibrating the silk of her nest. No vision involved; mating then happens inside |
| **Subadult, inside her nest** | Vibratory courtship, then he builds a second chamber onto her nest and **cohabits until she molts** to maturity |

That third tactic is worth sitting with. A wild male will move in with a subadult female and wait
for her final molt. It is also the tactic under which **cannibalism was never once observed**.

A nest here is a sheet of silk enclosing a hollow interior, usually with two doors — the same
structure a spider retreats into at night and molts inside.

## The cannibalism question

The reputation is largely undeserved, and the numbers are worth knowing before you talk yourself
out of pairing — or into being careless.

Across **1,834 recorded male–female interactions** in the laboratory, females killed and ate
males in **fewer than 1%**:

| Situation | Males killed |
| --- | --- |
| Female outside her nest | 1.08% |
| Female inside her nest | 0.56% |
| Male cohabiting with a subadult female | Never |

Only 14 females were ever cannibalistic, most killing a single male. In the field, over the whole
study, cannibalism was witnessed **three times**.

Two details that undercut the folklore further. Feeding did not protect a male, but nor did it
prevent mating — **12 of 32 females accepted a male while eating a fly**, and two accepted males
while feeding on a male they had killed earlier. And deliberately starving post-reproductive
females for 25 to 31 days to provoke the behavior **did not greatly increase it**.

It also runs both directions: in the same study two *males* killed and ate females, and males
killed other males more readily than females killed males.

### Where the evidence disagrees

A separate experiment on *Phidippus audax* — a different species, and reported so far only as a
conference abstract rather than a full paper — food-deprived 138 adult virgin females for 14 or
28 days and found the opposite: food-deprived females showed **more aggressive behavior, lower
mating success, and more cannibalism** than daily-fed controls.

So the honest position is that the effect of hunger is unsettled: one study found little, another
found some, on different species with different methods. Which is fine, because the practical
advice is the same either way and costs nothing.

**Feed the female well in the days before pairing.** Not because it is proven to protect the
male, but because it is free, and a well-fed female is a better bet under either result.

## Pairing in practice

Beyond the point above, the mechanics of a captive pairing are **keeper practice rather than
published protocol** — deliberately uncited here, because we have not found literature that
tests them. Treat this section as what experienced keepers do, not as established fact:

- **Introduce on the female's terms.** The published tactics all have the male approaching her
  space, not the reverse.
- **Watch the whole encounter.** Do not pair and walk away.
- **Have a way to separate them.** A piece of card is enough; you want it in your hand already.
- **If she is not receptive, remove him and try later.** A refused male can be offered again.
- **Do not pair a female who is in pre-molt.** She is not receptive, and a spider approaching a
  molt should be left alone regardless — see [molting](/care/molting).

## What happens next, and when

Once mating has happened, the timeline from the *P. johnsoni* work is remarkably regular:

| Stage | Time | Variability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Copulation → first eggs laid | **~25 days** | ± 5 days |
| Between successive fertile batches | **~30 days** | ± 14 days — much looser |
| Eggs laid → hatching | **~22 days** | ± 1.6 days — very tight |
| Hatching → spiderlings leave the nest | **~20 days** | ± 1.5 days — very tight |

The two tight numbers are the useful ones. **Once eggs are laid you have about three weeks to
hatching and about three more before the spiderlings disperse** — roughly six weeks from the
appearance of a sac to needing somewhere to put everyone. That is your real deadline, and it
barely varies.

Note also what the wide error bar on the second row means: a female who has not laid again after
six weeks has not necessarily finished.

## Signs it did not take

Mating is not a guarantee of fertile eggs, and you cannot tell from the outside.

- A mated female may lay **only infertile eggs**, or lay nothing at all.
- Even fertile females lay a substantial number of infertile eggs — around 42 each across their
  laying career, scattered through both fertile and infertile batches.
- **Once she lays an infertile batch, later batches were never fertile again.** An infertile
  batch is the end of the fertile series, not a blip in it.

There is no way to check a sac without destroying it. Waiting three weeks from laying is the
test.

## If you do not want to breed

Then the single most effective measure is simply **do not house adults together**, and be aware
that an egg sac still does not mean someone got in. An unmated female can lay a full clutch of
infertile eggs, and a wild-caught female can store sperm and produce a fertile sac months after
you acquired her. Both cases are covered in [egg sacs and gravid females](/care/egg-sacs).

Breeding deliberately is a reasonable thing to do. Breeding accidentally, and discovering the
arithmetic afterwards, is how spiderlings end up neglected.
