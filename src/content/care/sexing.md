Two signs settle it completely. Everything before those appear is inference, and the hobby is
full of confidently mislabelled spiders because of it.

## The two certain signs

**A mature male has clubbed pedipalps.** The pedipalps are the short appendages either side of
the mouth — mouthparts, not legs, as shown in
[where jumping spiders fit](/classification). In a mature male the tip of each is swollen into a
distinct rounded, polished knob, often described as looking like boxing gloves. This is the
**palpal bulb**, his copulatory organ, and it is only fully formed after the final molt. If you
can see it clearly, the spider is male. There is no ambiguity.

**A mature female has an epigyne.** This is a small hardened plate on the underside of her
abdomen — her external genital structure. You will find it sitting between the **book lungs**,
which are how a spider breathes: stacks of thin flat plates of tissue, arranged like the pages of
a book, which is exactly where the odd name comes from. They show as two paler patches toward the
front of the underside, and they make a useful landmark. Seeing any of this means looking at the
spider from below, which is far easier through the base of a clear enclosure than by handling.

Adult females are also almost always **larger** than males of the same species. In *Phidippus
regius* females average around 15 mm against 12 mm for males. Size alone is not proof, since a
well-fed male can outgrow a small female, but it corroborates.

## Why juveniles are hard

Because the definitive structure does not exist yet.

The male's palpal bulb develops on a specific schedule. Work on *Parasteatoda tepidariorum*
tracked it precisely: the bulb primordium first appears as a small ovoid organ inside the
pedipalp tip **towards the end of the pre-subadult stage**, and most of its development happens
during the **subadult stage** — the interval between the penultimate molt and the final one.

That gives three phases worth knowing:

| Stage | Pedipalps look like | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pre-subadult** | Slender, like a female's, perhaps very slightly thickened at the tip | Low — easy to get wrong |
| **Subadult** (after the penultimate molt) | Noticeably swollen and plump, but soft-looking and translucent, without a defined shape | High |
| **Adult** (after the final molt) | A hard, polished, clearly shaped bulb | Certain |

The subadult swelling is dramatic and worth recognizing, because it looks alarming if you do not
know what it is. It is not an injury or a parasite: the pedipalp tip is **inflated by haemolymph
pressure**, filled with fluid that rapidly coagulates while the bulb forms inside it. The
researchers compared the whole process to insect metamorphosis.

So if your spider's pedipalps suddenly look swollen and you have not seen this before, the
overwhelmingly likely explanation is a healthy subadult male developing exactly on schedule —
not an injury, a parasite, or a problem to treat. It is one of the most common false alarms in
the hobby.

**The species caveat here is a large one.** That study used *Parasteatoda tepidariorum*, a
cobweb spider — a different **family** from jumping spiders, not merely a different genus. The
broad sequence of pedipalp maturation is shared across true spiders, but the specific timings
are not transferable. The subadult stage lasted about 12 days in that species; do not assume the
same for a *Phidippus*.

## Earlier clues in Phidippus regius

Before the pedipalps say anything, *P. regius* gives some color and structural hints. These are
suggestive rather than definitive:

- **Males stay black and white throughout life.** Females are variable — brown, orange, tan,
  gray, or combinations.
- **Females may develop pale scales early**, reported from as early as the **third instar** in
  southern populations, while males remain dark and white throughout development.
- **Females have tufts of setae around the eyes** that males lack. *Setae* are bristles — spiders
  have no hair, so what looks like fur is a coat of stiff bristles, many of them sensory. Dense
  patches like these read as little eyebrows.
- **The chelicerae differ in adults.** The *chelicerae* are the pair of jaw-like appendages at the
  front of the face, each tipped with a fang — the part that looks like a moustache or a pair of
  tusks, and often brilliantly iridescent. In *P. regius* the males' are large, iridescent
  green-blue-violet, and carry small raised bumps (tubercles). Females' are iridescent green or
  red-violet, and smooth.

Two things to hold lightly. This is one species — do not carry it to *P. audax* or a zebra
jumper. And juvenile coloration varies enough between individuals and localities that a single
hint is not a diagnosis.

**Widely repeated in the hobby, and deliberately uncited here:** that *P. regius* becomes
reliably sexable around the penultimate molt, roughly the fifth instar. This matches the
developmental biology above and matches most keepers' experience, but no source for the specific
timing has been verified. If you have raised a clutch and tracked it, that is exactly the
observation this page is missing.

Note that this is a stage rather than an age. A warmer spider eats more and molts sooner, so it
will reach the penultimate instar in less calendar time than a cooler one — two spiderlings from
the same egg sac can be a fortnight apart on nothing but where their enclosures sit in the room.
Count molts rather than weeks, which is why the tracker records instars.

## How to actually look

- **Use the base of the enclosure.** Coax the spider onto a clear surface and look from
  underneath. This shows both pedipalps and the underside of the abdomen without handling.
- **Use magnification.** A phone camera's macro mode or a cheap loupe makes the difference
  between guessing and seeing. On a spiderling the structures are sub-millimeter.
- **Look after a molt, not before.** Colors are clearest post-molt, and any change in the
  pedipalps has just happened.
- **Check the exuvia.** The shed skin retains pedipalp shape and can be examined for as long as
  you like without disturbing the spider. This is the least stressful method available, and an
  underused one.

## Why it is worth knowing

- **Lifespan differs.** Males generally mature sooner and die sooner. Knowing you have a male
  reframes expectations about how long you have.
- **Males stop growing** at the final molt. A female may have molts ahead of her; a mature
  male does not, so the tracker's molt estimate no longer applies to him.
- **Egg sacs.** A female may produce one whether or not she has ever met a male — see
  [egg sacs and gravid females](/care/egg-sacs).
- **Pairing.** Any breeding attempt requires both to be confirmed mature, and mature males are
  at real risk during it.

If you are unsure, the honest answer is to record the spider as unknown and wait. The tracker
accepts "unknown" for exactly this reason, and one more molt usually settles it.
