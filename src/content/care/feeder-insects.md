Keeping a jumping spider means keeping insects too. [Feeding](/care/feeding) covers what to offer
your spider and how often — prey size, intervals, reading the abdomen. This page is about the
insects themselves: how to keep them alive, how long they last, and which ones cause trouble.

## The short version

| Feeder | Keeps for | Fridge? | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Flightless fruit flies** | 2–3 weeks per culture | **No** — cold stops production | Escapes; cultures crash without warning |
| **Blue bottle / house fly** | Weeks as pupae | **Yes** — the usual way to store them | Adults need something sugary once hatched |
| **Cricket** | Weeks, with work | **No** — they die | **They bite back.** Never leave one in overnight |
| **Dubia roach nymph** | Months | **No** | Weak feeding response — some spiders ignore them |
| **Mealworm** | Months as larvae | **Yes** — cold halts pupation | Hard shell, poor staple; some spiders can't manage them |
| **Waxworm** | 2–4 weeks | **Yes**, cool storage | Very fatty. Useful after a molt, bad as a routine food |

**Flies are the best all-round option** for a juvenile or adult jumper: the right shape, a strong
feeding response, and nothing that fights back. Most of the trouble on this page comes from the
other rows.

## Flightless fruit flies

*Drosophila melanogaster* and the larger *D. hydei*. The staple for spiderlings and small
species, and the culture most keepers end up running continuously.

**Keeping them.** Cultures are bought as a tub of medium already seeded. They produce for a few
weeks and then crash — the medium sours, and that is the end of it.

- **Start a new culture before the old one peaks**, not when it fails. Two or three staggered
  cultures means never being caught short.
- **Warm speeds them up, cool slows them down.** Room temperature is fine; a cold room stalls
  production entirely.
- **Never refrigerate a live culture.** It does not preserve it, it kills it.
- *D. hydei* is noticeably larger and slower to produce than *melanogaster* — worth keeping both
  once spiderlings start growing.

**Tapping them out.** Knock the culture down against the bench before opening, work over a sink
or in a closed bathroom, and expect some escapees anyway.

## Blue bottle and house flies

Bought as **pupae** (often sold as "casters" or spikes) rather than cultured, and this is what
makes them convenient: a tub of pupae in the fridge is a feeding schedule you can ignore for
weeks.

- **Store the pupae cold.** They sit dormant and hatch when warmed.
- **Warm a few at a time** — as many as you will use in a couple of days, in a pot they cannot
  escape from.
- **Adults need sugar.** A smear of honey or a scrap of fruit keeps them going; without it they
  are short-lived and thin.
- House flies are slightly smaller than blue bottles, which is useful for a smaller jumper.

## Crickets

Nutritionally reasonable and easy to buy, but the most work and the most risk.

House crickets (*Acheta domesticus*) take **two to three months to complete their life cycle** at
around 27–32°C, and will lay into any damp substrate — sand or peat — if you want them to breed.

**Keeping them:** a tall, smooth-sided, well-ventilated container; egg-carton or cardboard stacked
for surface area; dry food available constantly; and moisture from gel or vegetable rather than an
open dish, which drowns them.

**The thing to actually remember:** crickets chew. A cricket left loose overnight will gnaw on
your spider, and a spider that is molting cannot defend itself — [feeding](/care/feeding) is
emphatic about this for good reason, and [molting](/care/molting) explains why an interrupted
molt so often kills. Offer one, watch it get taken, and remove it if it is not.

They also smell and they are loud.

## Dubia roach nymphs

The easiest thing on this list to keep alive: they do not climb smooth walls, do not fly, do not
smell much, and tolerate neglect. A colony lasts months.

The trade-off is the feeding response. A roach that sits still does not always trigger a hunt, and
some jumpers simply ignore them. Worth trying; not worth relying on until you know your spider
takes them.

## Mealworms

*Tenebrio molitor*, and the numbers are worth knowing because they explain the storage trick.

On a standard wheat bran diet the **larval stage alone runs about two months**, the pupa about
**16 days**, and the adult beetle lives **three to four months**. Reared warm — around 25°C at
60–65% humidity — that whole sequence keeps moving.

**So: refrigerate them.** Cold holds the larvae in stasis and stops them pupating into beetles,
which is the entire reason a tub of mealworms keeps for months. Bring out what you need; keep
bran and a slice of potato or carrot in the tub for moisture.

**But use them sparingly.** The shell is hard, the nutrition ratio is poor, and smaller jumpers
often cannot deal with one. Not a staple.

## Waxworms

The treat food, and genuinely useful in one specific situation.

They are **very fatty**, which makes them a poor routine feeder but a reasonable way to put
condition back on a spider that has just come through a molt and needs to eat well. Store them
cool — not as cold as mealworms — and use them within a few weeks, because they will pupate.

**Not a staple.** A diet of waxworms is the feeder equivalent of living on chocolate.

## Why what the feeder eats matters

One finding is worth the detour, because it changes how much any of the above matters.

When spiders were fed prey manipulated to be protein-rich or lipid-rich, **the spiders' own body
composition tracked the prey almost exactly**, and they showed **no compensatory extraction** —
offered normal prey afterwards, a spider previously kept on a fat-heavy diet did not go looking
for protein to balance it. It simply ate what was in front of it. (That study used an orb-weaver
rather than a jumping spider, so the mechanism is likely general but untested on a salticid.)

Cultured insects are also consistently poorer than wild ones in **vitamin E and omega-3 fatty
acids**, and cultured crickets are low in **thiamine**. And because taurine sits in insect flight
muscle, **larvae like mealworms and waxworms contain little or none**, while adult flies and
crickets carry meaningful amounts — a second, independent reason the larvae are treats rather
than staples.

Two practical consequences:

- **Feed your feeders properly**, and give them something good 24–48 hours before they go in.
- **Vary the prey.** A single-species diet transfers that species' gaps straight into your
  spider, and your spider will not make them up elsewhere.

### The trap: almost all of this research is about reptiles

The feeder aisle is calcium powder and vitamin D3 dust. Nearly every gut-loading product and chart
you will find was developed for **reptiles and amphibians**, and it exists because vertebrates get
metabolic bone disease.

**Spiders do not have bones.** There is no published requirement to dust prey for a jumping
spider. Read that literature for what feeder insects *contain* — which is where the figures above
come from — and never for what a spider *needs*.

## Wild-caught prey

Free, and more varied than anything cultured. The cautions are **keeper reasoning rather than
measured risk** — we have found no studies quantifying this for pet spiders — but they are the
standard ones: pesticides from treated gardens and parks, parasites picked up outdoors, anything
hard-shelled or armoured, anything that stings, and brightly coloured insects as a class, on the
grounds that warning colours usually mean chemical defence.

Knowing the ground is unsprayed is the whole question.
