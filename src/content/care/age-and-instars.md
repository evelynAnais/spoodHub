Almost nobody knows how old their jumping spider is. If you bought it as a juvenile or found it
outdoors, its hatch date is unrecoverable — and it matters less than it feels like it should.

## Count molts, not weeks

**Instar is a better measure of development than age.** A spider kept warm and fed well reaches
its fifth instar considerably sooner than a cooler, leaner one hatched the same day. Two
siblings from one egg sac can end up a stage apart on nothing but where their enclosures sat in
the room.

So "fifth instar" says something useful about a spider. "Four months old" barely does.

That is why the tracker records instars rather than trying to compute an age, and why the
molting guide's interval table is indexed by instar.

## How many instars to maturity

Jackson's study of *Phidippus johnsoni* — same genus as a regal — followed spiders through their
whole life cycle in the laboratory and compared them against wild populations:

| | Males | Females |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Laboratory-reared** | 5–7 molts | 6–8 molts |
| **Wild (Coastal Range, California)** | 6–8 molts | 7–9 molts |

Three things in that table are worth dwelling on.

**Females take one to two more molts than males.** They mature later, end up larger, and live
longer as adults. If you have a confirmed female, expect more molts ahead of her than a male of
the same age.

**Wild spiders passed through more molts than laboratory ones.** Steady captive feeding pushes
a spider to maturity in fewer, faster stages. So a wild-caught spider may not follow the same
schedule as a captive-bred one from the same species.

**There is no fixed number.** Jackson found "considerable intrasexual variability" in adult
size, maturation time, and instar count. Two females of the same species can mature a molt
apart. Any table, including this one, is a distribution rather than a rule.

## Later instars last longer — and vary more

The single most practically useful finding: **instar duration, and the variability in that
duration, are both greater in later instars than earlier ones.**

A spiderling's stages are short and fairly predictable. An older juvenile's are long and
erratic. Which means:

- **Early on**, a rough interval table works reasonably well.
- **Later**, it does not. The spread widens faster than the average does.

This is exactly why the tracker switches from its default interval table to a spider's own
recorded history as soon as it has two molts to work from. A published average is least reliable
precisely when your spider is old enough for you to have data of your own.

It also means a "late" molt in an older spider is often not late at all. Before worrying, check
the [pre-molt signs](/care/molting) rather than the calendar.

## Estimating a spider you did not raise

You mostly cannot, and it is worth being honest about that rather than guessing.

What you *can* establish:

- **Adult or not.** This is the one hard landmark. A mature male has a visible palpal bulb; a
  mature female an epigyne — see [telling males from females](/care/sexing). Before that, no
  external feature pins a spider to a specific instar.
- **Roughly how far along.** Size relative to the species' adult range gives a crude bracket.
  A 6 mm *Phidippus regius* is clearly juvenile; a 20 mm one is clearly not.
- **How many molts *under your care*.** This is the number that actually matters, and the only
  one you can be certain of. Start counting the day it arrives.

What you cannot establish is calendar age. A wild-caught adult female might have matured last
week or months ago, and nothing about her appearance distinguishes those.

**The practical approach:** record the spider at "unknown" instar, log every molt from arrival,
and let its own history build up. Two molts in and the tracker has something better than any
table could give you.

## Maturity is a one-way door

The final molt changes what the numbers mean.

**Males stop molting at maturity.** Once the palpal bulbs are formed, that is the last moult he
will make — so molt intervals no longer apply to him at all, and the remaining question is how
long he lives, not how much he grows. Jackson found males have shorter adult lifespans than
females across the board.

**Females may have one or more molts left**, depending on species, and continue to grow.

This is the point where a keeper's expectations should shift. A mature male is not "overdue" for
a molt; he is finished.

## Old age

*Senescence* is the technical word for biological ageing — the decline that comes with age rather
than with illness or injury. There is nothing to treat, and nothing has gone wrong.

**Deliberately uncited:** what follows is widely reported keeper observation, not a published
finding. Jackson's work documents that adult females in seasonal habitats do not survive the
summer, but the signs of senescence in a captive spider have not been formally described in
anything I could verify.

Keepers consistently report that an old spider becomes:

- **less accurate when jumping**, sometimes missing easy targets or declining to jump at all
- **less interested in hunting**, taking prey slowly or ignoring it
- **more sedentary**, spending long periods low in the enclosure rather than up high
- **duller in colour**, without the molt that would normally restore it

The last one is the useful discriminator. Dulling colour with no molt following it, in a spider
already known to be mature, is age rather than pre-molt. See the
[molting guide](/care/molting) for how those two look different.

Handled gently, an old spider is not in distress — it is simply at the end of a fairly short
life. Keep water available, offer smaller and softer prey, and stop rearranging its enclosure.
