Jumping spiders are diurnal. They hunt by sight, so when the light goes they retreat into a silk
tube and stay there until morning. What happens inside that tube turns out to be more
interesting than anyone expected.

## What is actually known

In 2022 Rößler and colleagues recorded jumping spiders overnight and found something that looks
a great deal like REM sleep — the first such evidence in a terrestrial invertebrate.

Salticids cannot move their eyes, so they move their **retinas** instead, swivelling tubes
behind the lenses to redirect their gaze. The researchers found these retinal tubes moving in
regular bouts during night-time rest:

- bouts lasted a median of **77 seconds**
- they recurred roughly every **17 minutes**
- both the length of the bouts and the gaps between them **grew as the night went on** — bouts
  11% longer, intervals 38% longer
- every single bout was accompanied by **leg twitching and curling** toward the underside of
  the body — in 100% of observations
- none of it happened during ordinary waking movements like stretching or grooming

### Two honest caveats

**It was a different species.** The study used *Evarcha arcuata*, not *Phidippus*. Salticids
share the retinal-tube anatomy, so it is reasonable to expect something similar in a regal — but
that is an expectation, not a finding.

**"REM-like" is not "dreaming."** The paper documents a rest state with periodic eye movement
and twitching. Press coverage leapt to spiders dreaming; the researchers did not. Treat it as a
striking observation about rest, not a settled fact about spider minds.

There is also a methodological quirk worth knowing, because it explains something you may see:
the retinal movements were filmed in **spiderlings 1–9 days after emerging**, whose exoskeletons
are still translucent enough to see through. In a pigmented adult you cannot observe any of
this directly — only the leg curling.

## What it looks like in your enclosure

Come evening, a healthy jumping spider will usually:

- retreat into its silk tube, often near the top of the enclosure
- close itself in loosely — not the thick sealed chamber of pre-molt
- sometimes **hang from a single silk line**, legs drawn in, apparently suspended in mid-air

That last one alarms new keepers reliably. It is normal. Hanging on a dragline is a common
resting posture and not a sign of anything wrong.

## Resting leg curl vs. the one to worry about

This is the practical thing to take away. Curled legs mean two very different things, and
telling them apart saves a lot of panic.

The resting side of this is documented — the study above found leg curling accompanying every
rest bout. The "in trouble" side is **keeper knowledge, deliberately uncited**: it is
consistently reported across the hobby and matches what a dehydrated or dying spider does, but
no published study locates the distinction and no citable source for it has been verified.
Weigh it accordingly. If you know of a good one, it belongs in this page's sources.

| | Resting | In trouble |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Posture | Legs drawn loosely toward the body | Legs pulled tight underneath, rigid |
| Where | In a retreat or hanging on silk | Often on the floor of the enclosure |
| Response | Uncurls and moves off when disturbed | Little or no response |
| When | At night, or during a daytime rest | Any time |
| Recovers | Yes, within moments | No |

A spider that curls in a retreat at night and is patrolling again by morning is asleep. A spider
curled on the substrate that does not respond and stays that way is dehydrated, dying, or
already dead. Dehydration is the most common treatable cause — check the water situation first.

## Practical husbandry

**Leave them alone after dark.** Feeding, rehousing and enclosure maintenance all belong in the
daytime, when the spider is awake and can see what is happening. A spider startled out of rest
in the dark is more likely to bolt or fall.

**Give them a real day-night cycle.** Indirect daylight is ideal. Avoid keeping an enclosure lit
around the clock, and avoid a room where a lamp is on late every night.

**Do not use bright light to check on a sleeping spider.** If you must look, use the dimmest
light that works and keep it brief.

**A retreat near the top is what they want.** See the enclosure guide — anchor points high up
mean the spider builds somewhere it feels safe rather than wedging into a corner you need to
open.

## What is not known

Plenty. Whether the state is functionally sleep in the way vertebrates sleep, whether adults do
the same thing as spiderlings, whether species differ, whether disrupting it matters to the
spider's health — all open. If you have watched your own spiders at night and noticed patterns,
that is exactly the kind of observation worth adding as a tip below, because there is very
little published to compare it against.
