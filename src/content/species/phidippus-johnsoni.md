One of the largest and most frequently encountered jumping spiders in western North America, and
the best-studied *Phidippus* in the scientific literature.

## Why it appears on this site

Almost everything the [age and instars](/care/age-and-instars) guide says about how jumping
spiders develop comes from Jackson's 1978 life history of this species — the number of molts to
maturity, the difference between males and females, and the finding that later instars last
longer and vary more than earlier ones.

Because it is in the same genus as the regal jumping spider, that work transfers to the spiders
most keepers actually have far better than research on a different family would.

## Appearance

Both sexes have a **bright red abdomen** against an otherwise black body, and shining teal
chelicerae. The reliable difference is that **females carry a black central stripe** down the
red, while males do not.

That colour scheme is thought to mimic velvet ants — wingless parasitoid wasps with a famously
painful sting — which would make the resemblance a defence rather than a coincidence.

Adults run to about a centimetre, similar in both sexes, making this a mid-sized *Phidippus*:
smaller than a regal, larger than a zebra jumper.

## What Jackson's work established

Reared in the laboratory and compared against six wild populations:

- **Males mature in 5–7 molts, females in 6–8** — and in the wild, one more of each.
- **Females mature later, grow larger, and live longer as adults** than males.
- **Instar duration and its variability both increase** in later instars.
- **Roughly one month** passes between mating and the first egg sac, and another month between
  each successive sac.
- **Eggs hatch about three weeks** after being laid, and spiderlings leave the maternal nest
  about three weeks after that.
- Later egg sacs contain **fewer eggs, and fewer of them hatch**.

## Habitat

Relatively dry country — coastal dunes through oak woodland, from sea level to the tree line. It
builds tubular silk nests under rocks and wood, sheltering there overnight and in poor weather.

Jackson found the mating season varies sharply with climate: about two months in the Californian
Coastal Range, up to eight months in mild coastal habitats where adult females persist year
round, and restricted to the snow-free summer months at altitude.

## Keeping notes

**Captive husbandry is undocumented** — which is why this page carries no temperature or humidity
range. Jackson reared them successfully in a laboratory, but that is a research protocol rather
than a published care sheet, and this species is not kept commercially.

What can be said:

- **Prey to about half its own size**, per the feeding observations — a useful rule that matches
  the general guidance in the [feeding guide](/care/feeding).
- **A dry-country species**, unlike the humid-habitat jumpers, so ventilation likely matters even
  more than usual.
- **Females sometimes cannibalise males**, which is worth knowing before any pairing attempt.

If you keep this species, its husbandry sections are among the emptiest on the site despite it
being among the best studied — an odd gap, and one firsthand numbers would close.
