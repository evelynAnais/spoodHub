A tiny Mediterranean jumper, well known among arachnologists for the male's elaborate courtship
display and increasingly familiar in northern Europe as its range creeps upward.

## Why it appears on this site

*Saitis barbipes* is the species behind the defensive-behavior work described in
[behavior and body language](/care/behavior-and-body-language). Scriba and colleagues catalogued
how these spiders respond to a perceived predator — freezing, retreating, hiding, jumping down —
and found the response depends strongly on how close the threat is.

Their most quotable finding concerns males, and it is very much in character for this species:
when shown a predator, adult males frequently responded with **courtship displays** rather than
defense, and let threats approach considerably closer than females or juveniles did.

## Appearance

Males have a brown carapace with a light median band running back from the fovea, darker sides,
and a dark brown abdomen marked with an ivory median stripe that breaks into chevrons toward the
rear. Females are lighter — yellow-brown carapace, dark head plate, black eye region, and a beige
abdomen with black spotting.

The common name comes from the males' conspicuously coloured third legs, which they wave in
display.

## A range that is moving

Worth noting for anyone in northern Europe: this was a southern species until recently. It has
established in Belgium, the Netherlands and Germany within the last few decades. It is native to
Europe and spreading within it, not an introduced invasive.

## Keeping notes

**As with most wild European salticids, captive husbandry is undocumented** — which is why this
page carries no temperature or humidity range. What follows is what its natural history implies,
not measured practice.

- **Very small** — under 6 mm even for a female. Prey means *Drosophila melanogaster*, and
  enclosure ventilation has to be fine mesh.
- **Mediterranean**, so likely warmer and drier than a temperate species like
  *Evarcha arcuata* — but that is an inference from where it lives, not a specification.
- **The males are worth watching.** The courtship display is the reason this species gets studied,
  and it is elaborate enough to be visible without magnification.

If you keep this species, the husbandry sections here are the weakest on the site and would
benefit enormously from firsthand numbers.
